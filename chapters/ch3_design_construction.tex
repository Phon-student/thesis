\chapter{IMPLEMENTATION}
\label{chap:implementation}

This chapter presents the methodology and system design of the proposed Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework. The implementation is structured into primary phases: Robot to AI Communication, Dataset Preparation, Model Training, and Model Evaluation. This pipeline is designed to maximize sample efficiency while preventing hardware degradation during data collection.

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Phase 1: Robot to AI Communication}
\label{sec:phase1_communication}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The first phase establishes a robust and continuous data pipeline between the physical Dobot CR5 hardware and the AI processing backend. 

\subsection{TCP/IP Interface and Byte Decoding}
Data from the robot is transmitted via a TCP/IP interface as a continuous raw byte stream (e.g., \texttt{01001...}). To parse this stream, a custom \textbf{Byte Decoder} module is implemented. This decoder is responsible for unpacking the low-level data stream into distinct, usable states, specifically extracting the current 6-DOF joint poses and the binary gripper state.

\subsection{Tensorization and Storage}
Once the states are unpacked, they pass through a \textbf{Tensorization} layer. This step converts the raw numerical arrays directly into PyTorch tensors, ensuring the data is natively formatted for the machine learning pipeline. Finally, these tensors are grouped and written to a Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS) in HDF5 format, ensuring fast, parallel read-access during model training.

\section{Phase 2: Dataset Preparation}
\label{sec:phase2_dataset}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

To train the VLA model effectively without causing physical damage to the robot, the dataset collection strategy is split into simulation and real-world pipelines. The combined dataset is exported into the unified LeRobot schema.

\subsection{Simulation Data Pipeline}
Collecting large-scale repetitive trajectories on physical hardware often leads to mechanical wear, joint pain, and joint-lock issues during awkward or repeated poses. To circumvent this, the bulk of the dataset is generated in \textbf{IsaacLab}. Using a simulated Dobot CR5 configuration, an Inverse Kinematics (IK) and Event Generator drives the robot through randomized task configurations. An Observator module captures the rendered simulation data.

To ensure high-fidelity learning, the simulation pipeline enforces strict temporal synchronization across all modalities. As demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:example-sim-data}, the joint trajectories, gripper actuation states, and multi-camera rendered frames (overview and end-effector) are perfectly aligned at each timestep, providing the model with a coherent multimodal timeline.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/example_sim_data.png}
    \caption[Temporal synchronization in IsaacLab]{Temporal synchronization in the IsaacLab simulation environment. Joint trajectories (top), gripper states (middle), and multi-camera RGB frames (bottom) are precisely aligned across the episode timeline.}
    \label{fig:example-sim-data}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Real-World Data Pipeline}
To bridge the sim-to-real gap, a smaller subset of real-world data is collected using a two-step kinesthetic teaching method:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Record State Trajectory:} A human operator physically guides the robot arm through the task (kinesthetic teaching), and the TCP/IP interface records the precise joint state trajectory.
    \item \textbf{Replay Trajectory:} The robot autonomously replays the recorded trajectory. During this replay, the external and wrist-mounted cameras, along with other sensors, record the scene. 
\end{enumerate}
This strategy ensures that the visual data is perfectly aligned with the kinematics while remaining entirely free of human hands obstructing the camera views.

\subsection{Task Variance and Dataset Statistics}
The combined dataset encompasses three distinct task variances to teach the robot a complete pick-and-place sequence:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stay:} The robot learns to hold its position and maintain visual focus on the target object. This serves as a critical termination signal, teaching the robot to recognize when the "Reach" task is complete.
    \item \textbf{Reach:} The robot moves its gripper to a designated position directly above the target object.
    \item \textbf{Grip:} The robot executes the physical grasping mechanism to pick up the target object.
\end{itemize}

After merging the simulation and real-world pipelines, the final unified LeRobot dataset yields the following statistics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simulation Dataset:} 530 episodes, totaling 1 hour of continuous motion data.
    \item \textbf{Real-World Dataset:} 90 episodes, totaling 10 minutes of targeted, human-demonstrated data.
    \item \textbf{Total Task Variance:} 3 distinct tasks (Stay, Reach, and Grip) distributed across both domains.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Kinematic Analysis and Precomputed Motion Masking}
\label{sec:kinematic_analysis}
To optimize the training objective based on the physical realities of the manipulation tasks, an empirical kinematic analysis is performed on the collected dataset. Rather than treating all joints equally, the system identifies which joints are actively driving a specific trajectory chunk and which are merely idling.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/joint_analysis.png}
    \caption[Joints change distribution]{Empirical kinematic analysis of the dataset. A threshold of 0.001 normalized units/frame is used to independently classify each joint as moving or stationary.}
    \label{fig:joint-analysis}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:joint-analysis}, the per-joint moving proportion is analyzed across the dataset. A strict motion threshold of \textbf{0.001} (normalized units/frame) is established. Any joint velocity falling below this floating-point minimum is mathematically classified as stationary machine noise. Velocities exceeding this threshold are classified as intentional movement.

This binary classification (Moving vs. Stationary) is applied independently to each joint across all trajectory chunks, creating a dynamic motion mask (Figure~\ref{fig:joint-weighting}). To prevent computational bottlenecks during the live GPU training loop, these motion flags are precomputed offline during the data preparation phase and saved directly into the dataset tensors. This allows the loss function to apply targeted weights during training without any runtime overhead.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/joint_weighting.png}
    \caption[Precomputed motion mask application]{Application of the precomputed motion mask across trajectory chunks. Each joint is independently flagged as MOVING (orange) or STATIONARY (blue) to inform the weighted Position Loss.}
    \label{fig:joint-weighting}
\end{figure}

\section{Phase 3: Model Architecture}

The core system architecture processes multimodal inputs to generate fluid, safe robotic trajectories. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture}, the pipeline is divided into three primary stages: Perception, Action Generation, and the Safety Layer. At each control cycle, the system ingests a synthesized input tuple consisting of an RGB image, a natural-language instruction, and the current joint state. These inputs are processed by the cognitive backbone and passed to the diffusion expert, before final validation against hardware constraints.

\subsection{Vision-Language Backbone}
\label{sec:vlm_backbone}

The VLM Core utilizes the lightweight Qwen-VL architecture. While larger models (e.g., 4-billion parameters) are standard in large-scale VLA research, empirical benchmarking of spatial intelligence reveals that the smaller parameter models (2B) retain highly comparable spatial awareness. 

As detailed in Table~\ref{tab:spatial_intelligence}, the performance delta between the 4B and smaller variants across core spatial benchmarks such as RefCOCO (avg) and EmbSpatialBench is marginal. Consequently, downsizing to the smaller Qwen-VL backbone yields significant computational performance and memory footprint gains while sacrificing almost zero actionable accuracy for robotic manipulation. This backbone is responsible for extracting dense latent representations from the multi-camera visual inputs.

\subsection{Overview-Aware Strategy}
A critical challenge in end-to-end robotics is the tendency of models to "cheat" by over-relying on the end-effector (wrist) camera to align with an object, effectively ignoring the global scene context. To combat this, the architecture employs an \textbf{Overview-Aware Strategy}. During training, an \texttt{ee\_image\_dropout\_prob} of 20\% is applied, randomly blanking the end-effector view. This forces the model to extract spatial features from the overview camera, ensuring it understands the robot's coordinates relative to the entire workspace.

\subsection{Structured Latent Conditioning via Masked Cross-Attention}
\label{sec:latent_conditioning}

To bridge the high-dimensional semantic output of the QwenVL backbone and the continuous action-space requirements of the DiT, the architecture introduces a Perceiver-style cross-attention bottleneck. Rather than passing the entire sequence of VLM tokens directly to the diffusion expert---which would incur massive computational overhead and risk modality entanglement---the system compresses this knowledge into a fixed sequence of 64 trainable latent tokens. The data flow of this mechanism is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:model-flowing}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{images/model_flowing.png}
    \caption[Structured latent conditioning flow]{Detailed flow of the structured latent conditioning. A fixed set of trainable Latent tokens cross-attends to the QwenVL output. The refined tokens (Latent*) are combined with an orthogonally projected Robot State to form the final Context for the Diffusion Transformer.}
    \label{fig:model-flowing}
\end{figure}

A critical architectural innovation in this pipeline is the forced disentanglement of multimodal features. If the 64 latent queries ($Q$) are allowed to attend globally to all VLM keys and values ($K, V$), the model often collapses into a state where it primarily attends to a single dominant modality. To prevent this, the 64-token sequence is partitioned into four distinct chunks, and a custom boolean attention mask (Figure~\ref{fig:token-crossattention}) enforces strict routing rules during the cross-attention operation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chunk 1 (15 tokens):} Constrained to attend \textit{only} to spatial tokens originating from the overview camera.
    \item \textbf{Chunk 2 (15 tokens):} Constrained to attend \textit{only} to spatial tokens from the end-effector (wrist) camera.
    \item \textbf{Chunk 3 (10 tokens):} Constrained to attend \textit{only} to the natural-language instruction text tokens.
    \item \textbf{Chunk 4 (24 tokens):} Granted global attention to synthesize cross-modal contextual relationships.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/token_crossattention.png}
    \caption[Boolean cross-attention mask mapping]{Boolean cross-attention mask mapping. Shaded cells indicate permitted attention connections between the latent token chunks and the specific modality source tokens. Unshaded cells are masked.}
    \label{fig:token-crossattention}
\end{figure}

Before the DiT can utilize these visual and semantic features, the current 6-DOF joint and gripper state must be integrated. To map the physical state vector into the high-dimensional latent space without destabilizing the network, it is passed through a linear projection layer. Crucially, the weights of this linear layer are initialized using an \textbf{orthogonal projection strategy} relative to the mean of the physical state distribution across the training dataset. This technique prevents raw state features from dominating visual features early in training and ensures strict gradient stability. The projected state is then concatenated with the Latent* sequence to form the ultimate context tensor.

\subsection{Diffusion Transformer (DiT)}
The final context tensor is fed into the \textbf{Diffusion Transformer (DiT)} Action Expert. The DiT integrates these disentangled latent features with a noisy action input to iteratively generate the final predicted robot trajectory.

\section{Phase 4: Training Objectives and Flow Matching}
\label{sec:phase4_training}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Unlike traditional behavior cloning that predicts a single discrete action, this methodology utilizes \textbf{Flow Matching} for trajectory generation. The model learns to predict the "velocity" vector field that transforms a distribution of pure noise ($x_0$) into a distribution of expert trajectories ($x_1$).

\subsection{The Multi-Component Loss Function}
To ensure the generated trajectory is accurate, physically grounded, and smooth, the model optimizes a weighted combination of five distinct loss terms:

$$L_{total} = L_{flow} + \lambda_{pos}L_{pos} + \lambda_{smooth}L_{smooth} + \lambda_{div}L_{div} + \lambda_{state}L_{state}$$

\subsubsection*{Weighted Position Loss via Precomputed Masks}
To leverage the kinematic analysis conducted during data preparation (Section~\ref{sec:kinematic_analysis}), the Position Loss ($L_{pos}$) is not applied uniformly across all joints. Instead, it utilizes the precomputed motion masks to dynamically scale the loss gradients. 

The weighted Position Loss is formulated as a weighted Mean Squared Error (MSE) across all joints $j$:
$$L_{pos} = \frac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} w_j \| \hat{x}_{1,j} - x_{1,j} \|_2^2$$
where $\hat{x}_{1,j}$ is the predicted final coordinate, $x_{1,j}$ is the target coordinate, and the dynamic weight multiplier $w_j$ is defined by the motion flag:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $w_j = 2.0$ if Joint $j$ is classified as \textbf{MOVING}.
    \item $w_j = 0.8$ if Joint $j$ is classified as \textbf{STATIONARY}.
\end{itemize}
This academic rationale forces the network's gradient updates to focus heavily on correcting the joints actively driving the motion, rather than over-optimizing or hallucinating movements in idle joints.

\section{Phase 5: Model Evaluation and Improvement}
\label{sec:phase5_evaluation}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The final trained model undergoes a rigorous two-pronged evaluation protocol:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simulation Evaluation (Generalization):} The model is tested against unseen objects and novel environmental settings in IsaacLab to measure its zero-shot generalization capabilities.
    \item \textbf{Real-World Evaluation (Completion):} The model is deployed on the physical Dobot CR5 to measure the absolute object success rate across 10 trials per task. 
\end{itemize}
Insights gathered from these evaluations (specifically divergences in expected positioning and motion jitter) are fed back into the tuning weights for the Divergence, Smoothness, and Position losses to iteratively refine system performance.
