\section{Statement and Significance of the Problems}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The past decade has witnessed a profound transformation in artificial intelligence,
driven by the emergence of Vision-Language Models (VLMs). These neural networks jointly process
visual and textual inputs to produce semantically grounded outputs. By training on
hundreds of millions of image-text pairs, these models have acquired broad and
transferable understanding: they can recognise objects, infer spatial relationships,
follow complex instructions, and reason about scenes they have never explicitly been
trained on~\cite{openai2023gpt4v, liu2023llava, qwen3vl2025}. This generalized
understanding has successfully unlocked a new tier of software automation,
streamlining complex digital tasks such as zero-shot optical character recognition
(OCR) and automated document summarization~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}.

The natural progression of this capability is its extension from the digital domain
into the physical world. If a VLM can interpret an image and respond to questions
about it, it should be able to direct a robot to physically interact with the objects
it perceives. This transition forms the foundation of Vision-Language-Action (VLA)
models  systems that couple the semantic reasoning of a VLM with a physical action
generation module, enabling a robot to receive a natural-language instruction,
interpret the visual state of its environment, and produce motor commands to
accomplish the specified goal~\cite{brohan2023rt2, driess2023palme}. The potential
for industrial automation is substantial: a robot that understands language and
vision could be deployed flexibly across diverse tasks with minimal reprogramming,
adapting to new objects and instructions without the task-specific engineering that
classical robotic systems require~\cite{lavalle2006planning}.

However, while VLM research has flourished globally, the study and development of
VLA systems remains remarkably narrow. This disparity is primarily driven by severe
hardware and computational constraints. Although advanced VLA models such as
RT-2~\cite{brohan2023rt2}, OpenVLA~\cite{kim2024openvla}, and GR00T N1~\cite{nvidia2024groot} have demonstrated impressive results, their
development relies on resources that are inaccessible to most research groups:
multi-GPU server clusters, purpose-built robot fleets costing tens of thousands of
dollars, and datasets comprising hundreds of thousands of demonstration
episodes~\cite{openx2023, khazatsky2024droid}. As a consequence, VLA research
remains far narrower in participation and diversity than VLM research, and its
findings are difficult to reproduce or extend in resource-constrained settings.

This concentration of capability matters because it slows educational adoption,
weakens scientific reproducibility, and delays the transfer of embodied AI from
research laboratories to practical applications. If practical design principles for
VLA development under constrained conditions are not established, access to this
technology will remain limited to well-funded institutions. To address this, the
study proposes an end-to-end framework to develop a VLA model strictly on
consumer-grade setups. By bridging the gap between high-end capability and
low-resource accessibility, this framework aims to make VLA research reachable and
reproducible worldwide.

\newpage

\noindent The problem can be summarised in four concrete dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High entry cost}: existing VLA pipelines require long-duration data
    collection, large task diversity, and multi-GPU infrastructure that is beyond the
    reach of most academic groups and small research teams.

    \item \textbf{Insufficient low-resource methodology}: limited published guidance
    exists for training robust VLA models under narrow data and compute budgets, and
    no open end-to-end framework currently targets consumer-grade hardware.

    \item \textbf{AI-to-robot integration challenges}: practical deployment fails when
    model outputs cannot be reliably translated into safe, standardised, and
    executable robot commands at real-time control frequencies.

    \item \textbf{Incomplete validation}: many studies emphasise simulation
    performance while real-robot behaviour, zero-shot transfer, and deployment
    constraints remain under-reported, limiting the practical relevance of their
    findings.
\end{enumerate}

Addressing these issues is significant because it enables broader participation in
embodied AI research, improves scientific reproducibility, and increases the
practicality of language-conditioned robot systems for real operational environments
beyond large research institutions.



\section{Goal and Objectives of the Study}

The primary goal of this study is to develop a practical, end-to-end
Vision-Language-Action framework that operates effectively on consumer-grade
hardware, thereby democratizing access to embodied AI research worldwide. This is
achieved by designing a system that is explicitly bounded by consumer-grade
hardware constraints from the outset, rather than treating resource efficiency as a
secondary concern.

\noindent The specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To train a VLA model using the proposed end-to-end framework within an
    8~GB VRAM budget, and to demonstrate practical manipulation capability by
    achieving a task success rate above 50\%, improving clearly over the
    approximately 30\% baseline.

    \item To establish a robust VLA development framework that enables effective
    alignment between simulation data and real-world robot datasets, so that
    cross-domain learning, transfer reliability, and real-world deployment
    stability are improved under consumer-grade resource constraints.
\end{enumerate}



\section{Scope of the Study}


The study focuses on language-guided tabletop manipulation using a Dobot CR5 six-
degree-of-freedom robot arm. RGB visual observations from two fixed cameras and
natural-language instructions are the primary system inputs. The task domain covers
four manipulation categories: pick-and-place, stacking, drawer and container
interaction, and multi-step sequencing.

The demonstration dataset is intentionally constrained to a low-resource regime,
comprising approximately one hour of robot motion distributed across simulation and
real-world sources. Simulation trajectories are generated in Isaac Lab with domain
randomisation; real-world demonstrations are collected using the open-source Dobot
Dataset Collection Tools~\cite{falightsk2025dobot} via human teleoperation.

Model development and training are designed to fit commercially accessible compute
resources, with a target peak training budget of approximately 8~GB VRAM achieved
through bfloat16 mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, and QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora}.
Evaluation is conducted in both simulation and real-robot settings and includes
success-rate assessment on seen tasks, zero-shot generalisation analysis on unseen
object-instruction combinations, action validity measurement, and end-to-end latency
profiling.



\section{Process of the Study}


\noindent The study is conducted through a structured end-to-end process:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{System design}: a modular VLA architecture is specified by
    integrating three core stages  a QwenVL3.5 2B perception
    backbone~\cite{qwen3vl2025} for multimodal understanding, a trainable Diffusion
    Transformer Action Expert~\cite{peebles2023dit} for continuous trajectory
    generation, and a safety layer (constraint check plus protocol adapter) for
    verified robot dispatch. The architecture is designed to satisfy the 8~GB VRAM
    constraint from the outset.

    \item \textbf{Data construction}: simulation trajectories from Isaac Lab and
    real-world teleoperation recordings are collected, synchronised, and converted
    into LeRobot-compatible corpora with per-joint normalisation and outlier
    filtering. The simulation corpus is used for training, while real-world data is
    reserved for evaluation and deployment validation.

    \item \textbf{Model training}: the pipeline is trained in two stages. Stage~1
    trains only the lightweight connector and action modules with frozen backbones,
    establishing stable action generation before backbone adaptation. Stage~2 applies
    LoRA fine-tuning~\cite{hu2022lora} to the VLM backbone with reduced learning
    rate to adapt language-visual grounding to the manipulation domain without
    catastrophic forgetting.

    \item \textbf{Robot integration}: model outputs are translated into executable
    robot commands through a safety-aware protocol adapter that performs
    de-normalisation, constraint validation, and TCP/IP dispatch to the Dobot CR5
    controller. A three-tier failure-handling protocol (rescale, abort, episode
    reset) ensures safe operation under constraint violations.

    \item \textbf{Evaluation and analysis}: performance is measured across simulation
    and real-robot settings using four metrics  task success rate, action validity,
    generalisation ratio, and end-to-end latency. Ablation experiments isolate the
    contribution of each architectural module. Failure cases are categorised by stage
    (perception, language grounding, action decoding) to guide targeted improvements.

    \item \textbf{Iterative refinement}: error patterns identified from evaluation
    inform targeted improvements including data reweighting, LoRA rank adjustment,
    prompt engineering, and action-chunk horizon tuning, applied to improve
    reliability and deployment feasibility within the resource constraints.
\end{enumerate}



\section{Limitations of the Study}


Several limitations are acknowledged in this work. First, task diversity is
restricted to four tabletop manipulation categories using a small set of object
types; conclusions regarding task generalisation should not be interpreted as
evidence of broad universality across the full range of manipulation scenarios.

Second, the demonstration dataset is intentionally small approximately one hour
of motion data, with simulation-only training and real-robot deployment evaluation 
which may constrain robustness when facing highly diverse object configurations or
unconventional instruction phrasings not represented in the training distribution.

Third, the evaluation platform is restricted to a single robot type (Dobot CR5);
cross-platform transfer to robots with different kinematics, actuation mechanisms,
or control interfaces is not validated in the present study. Fourth, all experiments
are conducted under controlled laboratory conditions with consistent lighting and
known object categories. Large-scale industrial deployment, human-safety
certification, and long-horizon autonomous operation are outside the scope of this
thesis.

Finally, the VLM backbone is adapted through LoRA rather than full parameter
fine-tuning, which limits the degree of task-specific adaptation achievable within
the compute budget and may result in residual misgrounding on instruction phrasings
that differ substantially from the backbone's pre-training distribution.



\section{Definitions}


\noindent The following definitions are used consistently throughout this thesis:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Vision-Language Model (VLM)}: a multimodal neural network
    that jointly processes visual observations and textual inputs within a shared
    representational framework, enabling instruction interpretation, visual
    reasoning, and natural-language response generation~\cite{liu2023llava,
    openai2023gpt4v}.

    \item \textbf{Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model}: an extension of the VLM
    paradigm that additionally produces executable physical control actions from
    multimodal inputs, enabling an embodied agent to follow language instructions
    by directly manipulating objects in its environment~\cite{brohan2023rt2,
    kim2024openvla}.

    \item \textbf{End-to-end pipeline}: an integrated learning and deployment
    system in which perception, instruction understanding, and action generation
    are trained and executed as a single continuous process, without relying on
    manually engineered intermediate representations between stages.

    \item \textbf{Low-resource setting}: an operational condition characterised
    by constrained data volume and limited compute resources. In this study,
    the low-resource setting is defined as approximately one hour of robot
    motion data and a peak training and inference budget of approximately 8~GB
    VRAM on a single consumer GPU.

    \item \textbf{Zero-shot generalisation}: the capability of a trained model to
    perform correctly on tasks or instruction variants that were not explicitly
    observed during training, relying on the generalisation capacity of its
    pre-trained backbone rather than task-specific memorisation.

    \item \textbf{AI-to-robot interface}: the communication and safety layer that
    converts the continuous action vectors produced by the model into hardware-
    compatible robot commands, applying constraint validation and protocol
    formatting before dispatch to the physical controller.

    \item \textbf{Action validity}: the proportion of model-generated action vectors
    that pass all safety-layer constraint checks  joint limits, workspace bounds,
    speed limits, and format requirements  and are successfully dispatched to the
    robot controller without rejection.

    \item \textbf{Generalisation ratio}: the ratio of zero-shot task success rate
    to seen-task success rate, expressed as a percentage, used to quantify how much
    performance is retained when the model encounters object-instruction combinations
    not present in the training distribution.
\end{enumerate}
