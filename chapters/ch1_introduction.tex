\section{Background and Motivation}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The aspiration to create robots capable of operating autonomously in unstructured
human environments has been a defining goal of artificial intelligence and robotics
research for several decades. Early robotic systems relied on handcrafted control
programmes, explicit geometric models, and task-specific heuristics that yielded
reliable performance within narrowly defined operational envelopes. However, these
classical paradigms share a fundamental weakness: they are brittle in the face of
change. Introducing a previously unseen object, altering the lighting conditions of a
workspace, or issuing a novel instruction typically demands substantial re-engineering
effort~\cite{lavalle2006planning}. This brittleness has motivated the shift towards
data-driven, learning-based approaches to robot control.

Two parallel trends have recently converged to offer a compelling new direction. On
one side, the emergence of large-scale Vision-Language Models (VLMs) trained on
internet-scale multimodal corpora has demonstrated that neural networks can acquire
remarkably robust visual understanding, spatial reasoning, and natural-language
grounding without task-specific supervision~\cite{openai2023gpt4v,liu2023llava,bai2025qwen25vl}.
These models encode not merely perceptual features, but higher-order semantic
relationships that map intuitively onto the requirements of instruction-following robot
control. On the other side, advances in legged robotics have produced platforms
capable of operating across varied terrain, yet each morphology --- bipedal or
quadrupedal --- carries inherent trade-offs. Bipedal designs handle human-scale
environments and vertical obstacles naturally, but become dynamically unstable at low
speeds and during precise manipulation. Quadrupedal platforms provide superior static
stability and payload capacity, but struggle with steps, ladders, and fine-grained
manipulation tasks~\cite{kenneally2016design,bledt2018cheetah3}.

This thesis addresses both of these challenges simultaneously. The first challenge is
\emph{morphological}: designing a hybrid robot chassis that can switch between
bi-pedal and quadrupedal locomotion on demand, combining the advantages of both
configurations within a single affordable hardware platform. The second challenge is
\emph{interpretability}: despite the impressive capabilities of state-of-the-art VLMs,
their internal computations remain largely opaque. These models follow commands, yet
we cannot readily identify which latent features drive which robot actions --- a
fundamental barrier to trusted, safety-critical deployment~\cite{anthropic2023core,
bereska2024mechanistic}. An operator who cannot inspect the model's reasoning cannot
confidently suppress unsafe behaviours before live deployment in hospitals, care homes,
or disaster zones.

This project proposes a unified solution: a hybrid bi-pedal/quadrupedal robot paired
with an interpretable embodied-AI brain that uses Sparse Autoencoders
(SAEs)~\cite{templeton2024scaling,cunningham2023sparse} to discover and steer the
latent features governing the model's action selection. The AI brain is implemented
as a fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-2B-Thinking model~\cite{qwen3vl2025}, chosen for its
balance between multimodal capability and on-device inference efficiency (fitting
within a 24~GB VRAM budget). The interpretability layer decomposes the model's
internal activations into near-monosemantic features that can be audited, suppressed,
or amplified by operators at inference time without any model retraining.

\section{Problem Identification}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Based on a review of the existing literature, the following research gaps are
identified.

\paragraph{Gap 1: Morphological Rigidity in Legged Robots.}
Most commercially available and research-grade legged robots are committed to a
single morphology. Bipedal robots such as Boston Dynamics Atlas and Unitree H1 are
optimised for upright, human-scale locomotion but are energetically costly to stabilise
at low speeds and are poorly suited for tasks requiring a stable base during
manipulation~\cite{unitree2023h1,boston_dynamics2022atlas}. Quadrupedal robots such as
Unitree Go2 offer excellent stability and terrain adaptability but cannot easily
ascend stairs designed for humans or reach objects at human workspace heights.
Hybrid morphologies that switch dynamically between gaits are technically feasible
but have received limited attention in the context of affordable, open-source
platforms.

\paragraph{Gap 2: Inaccessibility of Natural-Language Robot Control.}
Deploying current robot systems still requires substantial domain expertise. Most
interfaces demand structured command sets, programmatic APIs, or professionally
designed user interfaces. Non-technical users --- caregivers, elderly users, field
workers --- are effectively excluded from operating robotic assistants. While VLMs
offer a natural-language interface in principle, integrating a VLM with a robot's
physical actuation layer in a lightweight, real-time manner remains a non-trivial
engineering challenge with few open-source solutions~\cite{brohan2023rt2,
driess2023palme}.

\paragraph{Gap 3: Opacity of VLM-Based Robot Controllers.}
VLMs deployed as robot controllers produce actions but do not expose their internal
reasoning in a form that human operators can inspect or intervene upon. Mechanistic
interpretability research has begun to address this gap for language-only
models~\cite{elhage2022superposition,bricken2023monosemanticity}, but applications
to \emph{embodied} AI systems --- where the model's outputs directly drive physical
actuators --- remain scarce. The inability to identify and suppress specific
behavioural features before deployment creates unacceptable risk in safety-critical
environments.

Building on these identified gaps, this thesis poses the following primary research
question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can a compact Vision-Language Model be fine-tuned to control a hybrid
bi-pedal/quadrupedal robot through natural-language commands, and how can Sparse
Autoencoders be applied to its internal activations to produce an interpretable,
operator-auditable embodied AI system?}
\end{quote}

The following secondary questions are also addressed:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
    \item What dataset composition and generation strategy is most effective for
    teaching a compact VLM to produce structured robot tool-call outputs?
    \item Can a TopK Sparse Autoencoder reliably identify monosemantic features
    within VLM hidden states that correspond to interpretable robotic behaviours?
    \item Does feature-level steering via SAE decoder vectors produce consistent,
    predictable changes in robot action outputs across a range of task types and
    steering strengths?
\end{enumerate}


%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Objectives}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The overarching objective of this research is to design, implement, and validate an
end-to-end embodied AI pipeline that addresses the research gaps identified above.
The specific objectives are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
    \item To design and fabricate a hybrid bi-pedal/quadrupedal robot chassis capable
    of switching locomotion modes, constructed from off-the-shelf servo motors and an
    embedded GPU compute module.

    \item To build a 10{,}000-record robot conversation dataset via an automated
    multi-threaded generation pipeline that pairs scene images with structured
    \texttt{call\_robot()} instructions, including multi-turn dialogue sequences.

    \item To fine-tune Qwen3-VL-2B-Thinking on the generated dataset so that the
    model reliably maps RGB scene images and free-form natural-language requests to
    valid \texttt{call\_robot()} tool-call strings.

    \item To train a TopK Sparse Autoencoder on layer-20 hidden-state activations
    harvested from the fine-tuned VLM, producing 32{,}768 interpretable, near-mono\-semantic
    feature dimensions.

    \item To demonstrate feature-level steering by injecting SAE decoder column
    vectors at layer~20 and evaluating the resulting changes in robot behaviour across
    a systematic strength sweep.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The scope of this research is delimited as follows. The physical robot targets
indoor tabletop and ground-level manipulation tasks; it is not designed for
outdoor terrain navigation or load-bearing applications. Visual observations consist
of a single RGB camera stream; depth sensing and stereo vision fall outside the scope
of this work. The VLM backbone is fine-tuned using LoRA adapters rather than full
parameter updates, which constrains the degree of task-specific adaptation but
preserves generalisation capacity and reduces compute requirements. The SAE analysis
is confined to layer~20 of the VLM; a comprehensive multi-layer analysis of the full
residual stream is left for future work. All experiments are conducted with the
robot either stationary or executing pre-planned servo trajectories; closed-loop
visual feedback during trajectory execution is not implemented in the current system.
Furthermore, the safety properties of the feature-steering mechanism are evaluated
qualitatively through controlled demonstrations; formal verification of safety
constraints is beyond the scope of this thesis.

\section{Contributions}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The principal contributions of this thesis are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
    \item \textbf{A hybrid bi-pedal/quadrupedal robot prototype} constructed from
    off-the-shelf components and an embedded GPU, capable of switching locomotion
    mode in response to natural-language commands. The hardware design, bill of
    materials, and control code are fully open-sourced.

    \item \textbf{An automated robot conversation dataset} of 10{,}000 JSONL records
    pairing EO-Data1.5M scene images with structured \texttt{call\_robot()} instructions,
    generated via a multi-threaded Gemini pipeline. The dataset covers single-turn
    commands and multi-turn task-planning dialogues, and is released publicly to
    support further research.

    \item \textbf{A fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-2B-Thinking model} that maps scene images and
    free-form user requests to structured robot tool-call outputs with end-to-end
    latency below two seconds per command cycle on a 24~GB VRAM device.

    \item \textbf{Trained TopK Sparse Autoencoder checkpoints} (sae\_task\_model.pt,
    sae\_agentic\_model.pt, sae\_topk\_production.pt) that decompose VLM layer-20
    activations into 32{,}768 interpretable feature dimensions, with Feature~\#5086
    identified as the primary task-planning neuron in the agentic model.

    \item \textbf{A feature-steering inference framework} that injects SAE decoder
    vectors at layer~20 via a \texttt{register\_forward\_hook}, enabling operators
    to amplify or suppress specific robot behaviours at inference time without
    retraining the model.

    \item \textbf{A Gradio web application} integrating image upload, natural-language
    instruction input, and a real-time steering-strength slider, making interpretable
    robot control accessible to non-technical users.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Organisation}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows.
Chapter~\ref{chap:litreview} reviews the theoretical foundations and related work
across five areas: legged robot morphology, Vision-Language Models, mechanistic
interpretability and Sparse Autoencoders, large-scale dataset generation, and
prior embodied AI systems.
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} presents the proposed system in detail, covering
the hardware design of the hybrid robot, the dataset generation pipeline, the
fine-tuning procedure, the SAE training curriculum, and the feature-steering
inference framework.
Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} describes the experimental setup and presents
the results of the fine-tuning evaluation, the SAE feature analysis, and the
steering sweep experiments, with qualitative and quantitative discussion.
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} synthesises the conclusions, revisits the research
questions, acknowledges the limitations of the current work, and identifies
directions for future research.
