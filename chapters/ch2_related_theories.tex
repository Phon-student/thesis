\chapter{RELATED THEORIES AND LITERATURE REVIEW}
\label{chap:litreview}

This chapter surveys the theoretical foundations and prior work most directly relevant
to the proposed system. The review is organised into five areas: legged robot
locomotion and hybrid morphologies, Vision-Language Models and their application to
robot control, mechanistic interpretability and Sparse Autoencoders, large-scale
dataset generation for robot learning, and a comparative analysis of prior embodied
AI systems that inform the design decisions presented in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

\section{LEGGED ROBOT LOCOMOTION AND HYBRID MORPHOLOGIES}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Bipedal Locomotion}

Bipedal robots are designed to replicate the upright, two-legged gait of humans,
enabling them to operate in environments built around human ergonomics --- doorways,
stairs, worktables, and manipulation zones at human workspace heights. Classic
control approaches for bipedal locomotion rely on Zero Moment Point (ZMP)
stability criteria~\cite{vukobratovic1972stability} and model-predictive
control~\cite{wieber2016modeling}, which plan joint trajectories such that the
robot's centre of pressure remains within the support polygon defined by its feet.
More recent learning-based approaches have demonstrated robust bipedal locomotion
over rough terrain through reinforcement learning in simulation followed by
sim-to-real transfer~\cite{siekmann2021blind,kumar2021rma}. Despite these advances,
bipedal platforms remain energetically costly and dynamically unstable at low speeds
or during quasi-static manipulation, where the narrow support polygon offers
little margin against external perturbations.

\subsection{Quadrupedal Locomotion}

Quadrupedal robots distribute their weight across four contact points, providing a
substantially wider support polygon and greater static stability. This makes them
well-suited to tasks requiring a stable base, rough-terrain navigation, and payload
carrying. Platforms such as Boston Dynamics Spot~\cite{boston_dynamics2022atlas},
Unitree Go2~\cite{unitree2023h1}, and ANYmal~\cite{hutter2016anymal} have demonstrated
reliable outdoor mobility. Learning-based locomotion controllers for quadrupeds have
achieved particularly impressive results: Hwangbo et al.~\cite{hwangbo2019learning}
demonstrated that an actuator network trained in simulation could transfer directly to
hardware, and Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2021rma} introduced Rapid Motor Adaptation, which
allows quadrupeds to adapt their gait online to unseen terrain properties. However,
quadrupedal morphologies are not well-suited to ascending human-scale stairs, reaching
elevated surfaces, or performing fine manipulation tasks that require upright posture.

\subsection{Hybrid and Reconfigurable Morphologies}

The complementary strengths of bipedal and quadrupedal morphologies motivate
the development of reconfigurable robots that can switch between locomotion modes.
Early work on reconfigurable robots demonstrated the feasibility of modular
self-reconfiguration~\cite{yim2007modular}. More recent platforms have demonstrated
practical mode-switching: the robot CENTAURO~\cite{kamedula2019centauro} combines a
quadrupedal lower body with an upper body capable of manipulation, while several
research groups have explored servo-based platforms that can adopt both biped and
quadruped stances through body morphology changes~\cite{kenneally2016design}. The
present work contributes to this thread by constructing an affordable, open-source
hybrid platform using off-the-shelf servo motors, and by pairing it with a
natural-language AI interface that allows the locomotion mode to be switched through
plain-English commands.

\section{VISION-LANGUAGE MODELS}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Transformer Architecture}

The Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention} is the foundational architecture
underlying all modern VLMs. It processes input sequences through stacked blocks of
multi-head self-attention and position-wise feed-forward layers. The core attention
operation computes a weighted aggregation of value vectors:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Attention}(Q, K, V)
    = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
    \label{eq:attention}
\end{equation}

where $Q$, $K$, and $V$ are linear projections of the input sequence and $d_k$ is
the key dimensionality. The self-attention mechanism allows every token position to
attend to all other positions, enabling the model to capture long-range dependencies
that recurrent architectures handle less efficiently. Large language models are built
by stacking many Transformer blocks and pre-training on internet-scale text corpora
via next-token prediction.

\subsection{Multimodal Extension and Instruction Tuning}

Modern VLMs extend the language Transformer to process interleaved image and text
tokens. A vision encoder --- typically a Vision Transformer (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}
trained with a contrastive objective such as CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} or
SigLIP~\cite{zhai2023sigmoid} --- maps raw image pixels into a sequence of patch
embeddings. A learned linear projector maps these embeddings into the language
model's token space, after which the image and text tokens are processed jointly
by the language backbone. Pre-training on large-scale image-text corpora, followed
by instruction tuning on curated demonstrations of instruction-following
behaviour~\cite{liu2023llava,openai2023gpt4v}, yields models that can respond to
complex compositional queries about visual content with high accuracy and
generalisation.

\subsection{Qwen3-VL-2B-Thinking}

This project adopts Qwen3-VL-2B-Thinking~\cite{qwen3vl2025} as the VLM backbone.
Qwen3-VL is the third-generation multimodal model in the Qwen series developed by
Alibaba Cloud. The 2B-parameter variant is specifically designed for efficient
on-device inference: it supports dynamic resolution image encoding via a Naive
Dynamic Resolution mechanism and processes image tokens using Multimodal Rotary
Position Embedding (M-RoPE)~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl} to preserve spatial relationships
across varying aspect ratios. The ``Thinking'' variant incorporates a chain-of-thought
reasoning capability, allowing the model to produce an internal scratchpad before
committing to a final answer, which improves performance on multi-step task-planning
requests. Critically, the 2B parameter count enables the model to run comfortably
within a 24~GB VRAM budget, making on-robot inference feasible without cloud
connectivity.

\subsection{VLMs for Robot Control}

The application of VLMs to robot control has emerged as an active research direction
under the umbrella of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models~\cite{brohan2023rt2,
driess2023palme,kim2024openvla}. RT-2~\cite{brohan2023rt2} demonstrated that a large
VLM pre-trained on internet data could be fine-tuned to output robot actions as
text tokens, generalising to novel objects and instructions seen only during
language pre-training. PaLM-E~\cite{driess2023palme} further showed that embodied
language models could integrate sensor readings, scene graphs, and natural-language
instructions within a unified sequence, enabling complex multi-step task planning.
The present work follows this paradigm but targets a compact model suitable for
on-device deployment, and introduces interpretability mechanisms that these prior
systems lack.

\section{MECHANISTIC INTERPRETABILITY AND SPARSE AUTOENCODERS}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{The Superposition Hypothesis}

A fundamental obstacle to understanding neural networks is the superposition
hypothesis~\cite{elhage2022superposition}: the claim that neural networks store far
more features than they have neurons, by representing multiple features as
overlapping linear combinations in the same set of activations. A neuron in a
superposed network is not monosemantic --- it does not respond to a single,
interpretable concept --- but rather polysemantic, activating for several unrelated
inputs. This polysemanticity is the primary reason that simply inspecting individual
neuron activations does not yield human-interpretable explanations of model
behaviour.

\subsection{Sparse Dictionary Learning and Sparse Autoencoders}

Sparse dictionary learning offers a principled solution to the superposition problem.
If a model's activations can be expressed as sparse linear combinations of a larger
set of basis vectors (a ``dictionary''), then each basis vector can be
interpretable even if the original neurons are not. A Sparse Autoencoder (SAE)
implements this decomposition as a neural network~\cite{olshausen1997sparse,
cunningham2023sparse}: an encoder maps an activation vector $\mathbf{h}$ to a
sparse code $\mathbf{f}$, and a decoder reconstructs the activation from the sparse code:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f} = \text{TopK}\!\left(W_{\text{enc}}\,\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_{\text{enc}}\right),
    \qquad
    \hat{\mathbf{h}} = W_{\text{dec}}\,\mathbf{f} + \mathbf{b}_{\text{dec}}
    \label{eq:sae}
\end{equation}

where $W_{\text{enc}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sae}} \times d_{\text{model}}}$,
$W_{\text{dec}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{sae}}}$, and TopK
retains only the $k$ largest activations, enforcing sparsity. The SAE is trained to
minimise the reconstruction loss $\|\mathbf{h} - \hat{\mathbf{h}}\|_2^2$ subject to
the sparsity constraint imposed by TopK. In the TopK formulation~\cite{gao2024scaling},
the sparsity level $k$ is fixed as a hyperparameter, which produces more predictable
and controllable activation patterns than the $L_1$ penalty used in earlier
variants~\cite{cunningham2023sparse}.

Because the dictionary size $d_{\text{sae}}$ is made much larger than the model
dimension $d_{\text{model}}$ (an ``expansion factor'' of 16$\times$ in this work,
giving $d_{\text{sae}} = 32{,}768$ for $d_{\text{model}} = 2{,}048$), individual SAE
features can afford to be sparse --- activating only for a narrow, specific input
distribution --- and are therefore more likely to be monosemantic.

\subsection{Feature Steering}

Once interpretable features have been identified, the columns of the SAE decoder
matrix $W_{\text{dec}}$ provide a natural set of ``steering vectors'' that can be
added to the model's residual stream at inference time to amplify or suppress the
corresponding feature~\cite{turner2023activation,templeton2024scaling}. The steering
operation at layer $\ell$ is:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{\text{steered}}
    = \mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} + \alpha \cdot \mathbf{d}_i
    \label{eq:steering}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{d}_i = W_{\text{dec}}[:, i]$ is the decoder column for feature $i$
and $\alpha$ is a scalar steering strength. This intervention does not require
gradient computation or model retraining; it is applied at inference time via a
forward hook registered on the target layer. The technique was introduced in the
context of language models~\cite{turner2023activation} and has since been applied
to bias, safety, and persona-related
features~\cite{templeton2024scaling,anthropic2023core}. The present work extends
feature steering to the domain of \emph{embodied AI}, where the steered features
influence physical robot action outputs rather than text generation.

\subsection{Mechanistic Interpretability in Language Models}

The broader field of mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer the
computations performed by neural networks at the level of individual components.
Foundational work by Elhage et al.~\cite{elhage2021mathematical} provided a
mathematical framework for understanding transformers in terms of residual streams,
attention heads, and MLP sublayers. Subsequent work identified induction
heads~\cite{olsson2022context}, which implement in-context learning, and circuits
for factual recall~\cite{meng2022rome} and indirect object
identification~\cite{wang2022interpretability}. Bricken et al.~\cite{bricken2023monosemanticity}
applied sparse autoencoders to a one-layer MLP and demonstrated that individual SAE
features corresponded to highly specific, interpretable input patterns. Templeton
et al.~\cite{templeton2024scaling} scaled this analysis to Claude Sonnet, identifying
millions of human-interpretable features. The present work applies the SAE methodology
to an embodied AI context, examining features in the robot action planning layer of a
fine-tuned VLM.

\section{DATASET GENERATION FOR ROBOT LEARNING}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Open Robot Demonstration Datasets}

Large-scale robot demonstration datasets have become a key driver of progress in
robot learning. Open X-Embodiment~\cite{openx2023} aggregated demonstrations from
22 robot platforms across more than 500 tasks, demonstrating that co-training on
heterogeneous datasets improves generalisation. DROID~\cite{khazatsky2024droid}
collected 76,000 demonstrations across diverse environments, while
BridgeData~\cite{walke2023bridgedata} provided 60,000 tabletop manipulation
trajectories across a wide range of objects and instructions. These datasets provide
pre-training corpora for VLA models but do not inherently produce the structured
tool-call instruction format required by the present system.

\subsection{Synthetic Dataset Generation with Large Language Models}

A growing body of work explores the use of large language models to generate
synthetic training data for downstream tasks~\cite{wang2022self,gunasekar2023textbooks}.
In the robot domain, Brohan et al.~\cite{brohan2023rt2} used internet-scale data
to augment physical demonstrations, while more recent work has employed LLMs to
generate task descriptions, scene annotations, and reward functions for robot
learning pipelines~\cite{liang2023code,ahn2022saycan}. The present work contributes
a fully automated multi-threaded dataset generation pipeline that uses Gemini
(gemma-3-27b-it) to produce 10{,}000 structured \texttt{call\_robot()} conversation
records from EO-Data1.5M scene images, including single-turn commands and multi-turn
task-planning dialogues with visual verification steps.

\subsection{Instruction Tuning and Tool-Call Fine-Tuning}

Instruction tuning~\cite{wei2021finetuned,ouyang2022instructgpt} adapts pre-trained
language models to follow human instructions by fine-tuning on curated
instruction-response pairs. Tool-call fine-tuning is a specialised form of
instruction tuning that teaches models to produce structured function-call outputs
that can be parsed and executed programmatically~\cite{schick2024toolformer,
patil2023gorilla}. The \texttt{call\_robot()} format used in this work is directly
analogous to the tool-call format of function-calling APIs, framing robot commands
as structured JSON-like function calls that can be parsed reliably by a downstream
execution module.

\section{RELATED EMBODIED AI SYSTEMS}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{SayCan}

SayCan~\cite{ahn2022saycan} was an early demonstration of grounding a large language
model in robotic capabilities by combining the model's semantic knowledge with
value functions that estimate the feasibility of each skill in the current state.
The LLM proposes candidate actions in natural language; the value functions score
each action's physical plausibility; and the highest-scoring action is executed.
SayCan demonstrated that language models could enable long-horizon task planning
for robots, but it required pre-specified skill libraries and could not adapt to
novel manipulation scenarios.

\subsection{Code as Policies}

Liang et al.~\cite{liang2023code} proposed using LLMs to generate robot control
code directly from natural-language instructions, with the generated code invoking
a library of perception and motor primitives. This approach leverages the code
generation capabilities of large language models and produces interpretable,
executable robot programs. The \texttt{call\_robot()} format adopted in the present
work shares this philosophy: the VLM generates a structured function call that is
parsed and dispatched to the robot's hardware abstraction layer.

\subsection{OpenVLA}

OpenVLA~\cite{kim2024openvla} is an open-source VLA model that established
widely-adopted conventions for combining a VLM backbone with robot action prediction.
It employs a dual-encoder vision backbone (SigLIP + DINOv2) and produces robot
actions as discrete token sequences via next-token prediction. OpenVLA demonstrated
that fine-tuning a general-purpose VLM on robot demonstrations yields substantially
better generalisation than task-specific baselines. However, it does not expose any
interpretability mechanisms, and its action tokenisation targets continuous 6-DOF
manipulation rather than the discrete locomotion-mode commands addressed in the
present work.
