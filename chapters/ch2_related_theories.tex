
\chapter{RELATED THEORIES AND LITERATURE REVIEW}
\label{chap:litreview}

This chapter synthesizes the theoretical and empirical foundations that motivate the
proposed Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework. Rather than surveying prior work in
isolation, the chapter connects core theories to the concrete problem addressed in
this thesis: building practical natural-language robot manipulation under limited data
and constrained compute.

The discussion is organized into five themes: (1) limitations of classical robot
pipelines, (2) foundations of Vision-Language Models (VLMs), (3) action modeling for
manipulation, (4) integrated VLA system design in recent literature, and (5)
generalization and transfer challenges. These themes form the conceptual bridge from
problem statement (Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}) to methodology
(Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}).


%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{LIMITATIONS OF CLASSICAL ROBOT SYSTEMS}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Classical robot systems are typically composed of hand-engineered perception,
planning, and control modules. This design provides predictable behavior in fixed
conditions, but it is often task-specific and difficult to scale to open-world
settings. Changes in objects, layouts, or command style can require redesign of
features, rules, and control logic.

A major weakness is limited generalization. Policies tuned for narrow task templates
frequently fail in unseen scenarios, especially when visual context and language
instructions vary simultaneously. In practical terms, this leads to high maintenance
cost, brittle behavior outside curated test distributions, and poor accessibility for
non-expert users.

Another structural limitation is interface rigidity. Classical systems commonly depend
on deterministic command schemas or finite-state interfaces that assume predefined task
structure. Such assumptions conflict with natural-language interaction, where intent is
often underspecified, rephrased, or context dependent. This gap motivates multimodal
approaches that can jointly interpret language and visual context before action
generation.

\section{VISION-LANGUAGE MODEL FOUNDATIONS}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Transformer Backbone}

Modern VLMs are built on the Transformer architecture~\cite{vaswani2017attention},
which models long-range dependencies using multi-head self-attention. Given query,
key, and value matrices, the core attention operation is:

\begin{equation}
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\end{equation}

where \(d_k\) is the dimension of the key vectors. Multi-head attention computes
attention in parallel across multiple representation subspaces, allowing the model to
attend to different semantic aspects simultaneously. Each attention head specializes in
different feature relationships, and outputs are concatenated and projected to form
the final multi-head attention output.

This mechanism supports flexible context integration across image and language tokens.
For embodied decision-making, this property is particularly relevant because robot
policies must condition on long contextual chains: instruction text, scene structure,
task history, and safety constraints. Attention-based architectures provide a practical
mechanism for such cross-token dependency modeling, especially when perception and
language are encoded in a shared sequence space.

Transformer layers further employ feed-forward networks, layer normalization, and
residual connections to stabilize training and enable deep networks. In the context
of VLMs for robotics, these architectural properties enable encoder-decoder VLA
structures where image and text inputs are jointly encoded into a shared embedding
space before being decoded into action predictions.

\subsection{Vision-Language Instruction Following}

Recent VLMs demonstrate strong multimodal instruction-following behavior through
large-scale pretraining and instruction tuning~\cite{openai2023gpt4v,liu2023llava}.
Typical encoders use dual-stream architectures: a vision encoder (e.g., DINOv2 or
CLIP-based models) processes RGB images into spatial feature maps, while a language
encoder (often a pretrained LLM) encodes natural-language instructions into token
embeddings. These streams are fused through projection layers or cross-attention
mechanisms to create a unified multimodal representation.

For robotic systems, this capability enables grounding free-form user requests in
scene context, reducing the need for rigid command interfaces. The alignment between
visual and linguistic semantics learned during pretraining carries over to robotics
domains through transfer learning.

However, VLM output must still be translated into robot-executable actions with low
latency and high reliability, which motivates explicit action modeling beyond
language generation alone. From a systems perspective, two critical observations are:
First, language-level correctness does not imply control-level correctness; syntactically
coherent language may still ground to infeasible or unsafe motions. Second, VLM
confidence scores or softmax probabilities over language tokens are not calibrated to
robot execution safety. Therefore, practical robotic deployment requires an intermediate
control layer with explicit validation rules, workspace checking, and kinematic
compatibility verification before hardware dispatch.

\section{ACTION MODELING FOR ROBOT MANIPULATION}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Action generation for manipulation usually requires a design choice between discrete
and continuous representations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Discrete actions} are easier to validate and parse but may reduce
    motion fidelity.
    \item \textbf{Continuous actions} allow fine-grained control but are more
    sensitive to model errors and deployment noise.
\end{itemize}

In practice, many systems use structured or mixed forms to balance robustness and
expressiveness. For real-time control, action validity and execution safety are as
important as semantic correctness.

In low-resource settings, this representation choice becomes more critical. With
limited demonstration diversity, purely continuous policies may overfit trajectory
statistics and degrade sharply in unseen configurations. Hybrid action schemas, where
high-level decisions are discretized (e.g., grasp, place, release) and low-level
motion remains continuous, can improve controllability while preserving enough
dexterity for manipulation tasks.

\subsubsection{Autoregressive Action Generation}

Autoregressive models (exemplified by OpenVLA~\cite{kim2024openvla}) generate action
sequences token-by-token, conditioned on previously generated actions and multimodal
context. This approach enables direct attention to grounded linguistic intent at each
decoding step. The advantage is computational simplicity and straightforward likelihood
maximization during training. A limitation is that errors in early time-steps can
propagate, potentially producing incoherent long-horizon trajectories.

\subsubsection{Diffusion-Based Action Generation}

Diffusion models (as in Pi 0.5~\cite{sun2025pi05} and enhanced DiT
architectures~\cite{peebles2023dit,liu2024enhanceddit}) operate by learning to
iteratively refine action trajectories from random noise via a learned reverse diffusion
process. The forward diffusion process gradually adds noise to clean trajectories, and
the model learns to predict and remove noise conditioned on multimodal inputs. Key
advantages include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trajectory smoothness}: iterative refinement naturally encourages
    spatiotemporal coherence, reducing jerky or discontinuous joint motion.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty modeling}: the diffusion process naturally captures
    multimodal action distributions, important when multiple valid action sequences
    satisfy the same instruction.
    \item \textbf{Long-horizon stability}: refinement steps reduce accumulation of
    single-step prediction errors over long horizons.
\end{itemize}

For low-resource robotics, diffusion-based approaches have shown particular promise
because they can be implemented efficiently with compact DiT (Diffusion Transformer)
backbones that fit within 8 GB VRAM constraints while maintaining strong performance.

Action modeling in this thesis therefore emphasizes three properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
    \item \textbf{Executability}: model outputs must map directly to controller-valid
    commands.
    \item \textbf{Safety compatibility}: outputs must satisfy workspace and joint-range
    constraints before execution.
    \item \textbf{Latency feasibility}: decoding and validation must remain practical
    for interactive command cycles (a critical constraint for 8 GB VRAM deployment).
\end{enumerate}

\section{VISION-LANGUAGE-ACTION INTEGRATION}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{VLA Systems in Prior Work}

Research on Vision-Language-Action integration has shown that combining multimodal
reasoning with action prediction can improve robotic adaptability. Foundational work
included RT-2~\cite{brohan2023rt2} for language-conditioned action generation and
PaLM-E~\cite{driess2023palme} for embodied multimodal context integration.

\subsubsection{Architectural Landscape and SOTA Approaches}

More recent open-source efforts have accelerated the field. OpenVLA~\cite{kim2024openvla}
provided an accessible framework for scalable VLA training via an autoregressive
architecture, establishing a dual-encoder convention (SigLIP for vision, CLIP for
language) that has become widely adopted. The architectural landscape has since
expanded to include diffusion-based approaches: Pi 0.5~\cite{sun2025pi05} demonstrates
state-of-the-art performance using diffusion policy methodology with careful action
space design for dexterity-task transfer, while CoBot~\cite{lin2025sota} establishes
a practical open-source pipeline for real-world deployment with modular action
validation layers.

These systems indicate that end-to-end multimodal-action learning can outperform
strictly task-specific pipelines, particularly when data diversity is sufficiently
high. At the same time, practical deployment still faces significant constraints:
in compute and memory (especially for 8 GB VRAM class hardware), latency requirements
for interactive control, data scale limitations in low-resource settings, and module
coordination complexity. The choice between autoregressive and diffusion-based action
generation remains architecture-dependent, with diffusion models increasingly preferred
for trajectory smoothness, multimodal action handling, and uncertainty quantification
under deployment constraints.

An important methodological implication is that integration quality is not solely a
model-size problem. Even strong backbones can fail if perception features, language
intent representations, and action outputs are weakly coupled. Consequently,
architectural clarity and interface design are central to robust VLA behavior.

\subsection{Need for Modular Integration}

A practical VLA system must synchronize three capabilities:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
    \item language grounding and task understanding,
    \item visual feature extraction under scene variation,
    \item robust generation of executable manipulation actions.
\end{enumerate}

This motivates the modular design used in this thesis: VLM core, Vision Expert, and
Action Expert connected by explicit intermediate representations.

The modular strategy is adopted not as a rejection of end-to-end learning, but as a
practical compromise between trainability, interpretability, and computational
efficiency. It enables targeted diagnosis of failure sources (language grounding versus
visual ambiguity versus action decoding) while supporting end-to-end optimization during
later refinement stages. Explicit intermediate representations also improve debugging and
allow module-specific optimization: the VLM can be tuned for instruction grounding
accuracy, the Vision Expert for task-relevant perception, and the Action Expert for
target smoothness and executability independently, before final integration testing.

This approach is particularly valuable in low-resource settings where full end-to-end
retraining is expensive. Modular interfaces also facilitate sim-to-real transfer by
allowing targeted domain adaptation of individual modules (e.g., retraining the Vision
Expert on real camera data while keeping other modules frozen).

\section{GENERALIZATION IN ROBOT LEARNING}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Generalization remains a central challenge in embodied AI. Training performance on
seen tasks does not guarantee transfer to unseen objects, compositions, and
instruction phrasings. Dataset limitations amplify this gap because robot
demonstrations are expensive to collect at scale.

Current literature suggests several factors that improve transfer:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Task diversity}: broader coverage of manipulation primitives
    (grasping, placing, pushing, stacking) and object categories during training.
    \item \textbf{Language variation}: diverse phrasings and natural-language
    descriptions of the same task reduce linguistic overfitting.
    \item \textbf{Visual robustness}: vision encoders pretrained on large-scale
    datasets (e.g., ImageNet, LAION) or trained with self-supervised objectives
    (e.g., DINOv2) tend to generalize better across lighting, camera angles, and
    object appearance.
    \item \textbf{Interface stability}: stable, hardware-compatible action spaces
    reduce execution noise and enable smoother generalization to novel configurations.
    \item \textbf{Sim-to-real transfer}: training on simulation with domain randomization
    and then fine-tuning on limited real-world data often outperforms
    simulation-only approaches.
\end{itemize}

These findings directly inform the design constraints and evaluation protocol of this
thesis, where unseen-task performance is treated as a primary metric rather than a
secondary result. Specifically, the thesis maintains separate evaluation of seen and
unseen tasks to measure generalization quality explicitly.

For low-resource VLA development, generalization capability should be interpreted as
a transfer ratio (unseen performance divided by seen performance), not only as an
absolute competence score. The key question is: how much capability is retained when
moving from seen to unseen conditions under fixed resource budgets? This contextual
view aligns with the thesis objective of practical deployment rather than leaderboard
optimization. A system that achieves 80\% success on seen tasks and 70\% on unseen
tasks (87.5\% retention ratio) may be more practically useful than one achieving 95\%
on seen but only 50\% on unseen (52.6\% retention ratio), because the former is more
reliable for new user requests.

\section{CHAPTER SUMMARY}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This chapter established the theoretical and empirical basis for the proposed
Vision-Language-Action approach. Prior work confirms the potential of multimodal
robot learning but also highlights unresolved issues in flexibility, natural-language
interaction, and generalization. These gaps motivate the VLA architecture developed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, where a VLM, Vision Expert, and Action Expert are
integrated for practical robot manipulation under real-world constraints.
